## The problem

A mid-size clothing retailer had a returns page that worked. You could start a
return in four taps, print a label, and drop the parcel off. By every metric the
team tracked, it was fine: completion rate 91%, no drop-off spike, no bug
reports.

Support told a different story. Returns generated 38% of all tickets, and 80% of
those tickets were the same question in different words: *where is my refund?*
Nobody was stuck in the flow. They were stuck after it.

## Constraints

Six weeks, one designer, one engineer at half time. The warehouse system was a
decade-old third-party service we could read from but not change, and it only
reported one thing: whether a parcel had been scanned at intake. Legal would not
let us state a refund date we could not guarantee. And the flow itself was not
allowed to get longer — the previous team had cut it from seven steps to four
and that was a result nobody wanted to hand back.

## What I did

I read tickets before I drew anything. Two hundred of them, tagged by what the
customer actually wanted to know. Three clusters came out, and the biggest was
not "where is my parcel" — it was "did you get it, and is that the end of it?"

Then I sat with four support agents while they answered live. Every one of them
had a saved reply that said, roughly: *we've got it, refunds take 5–7 working
days from the scan, you don't need to do anything else.* They were sending that
sentence forty times a day. It existed. It just lived in a support tool instead
of on the site.

I ran five sessions with recent returners, showing the existing confirmation
screen and asking what they expected to happen next. Four of the five said they
would check their bank in a couple of days and email if nothing showed. The
fifth said she'd already emailed twice, "just to be safe."

## Decisions, and why

**Status, not tracking.** The obvious build was a tracking page. We had no data
to fill it with — one scan event is not a timeline — and a tracking page that
says nothing for four days makes anxiety worse, not better. We showed a state
instead: *received, refund on its way, 5–7 working days.* Three states, each
with a plain-language line about what happens next and what the customer needs
to do, which was always nothing.

**An email at the scan, not at the request.** The confirmation email went out
when someone started a return, which is the moment they are least worried. The
one that mattered was the scan at the warehouse — the moment we could honestly
say *we have it.* Moving the email cost the engineer an afternoon.

**A range we could keep.** Legal wouldn't approve a date. They approved
"5–7 working days from when we scan it," because it was true and we could point
at the scan. Vaguer than I wanted, specific enough to stop the second email.

## Outcome

Returns tickets fell 46% in the eight weeks after launch. The flow itself did
not change: same four taps, same completion rate. What changed was one email and
one screen after it.

The number I care about more is that the saved reply the support team used forty
times a day now gets used about four times a week.

## What I'd change

I spent the first week and a half redesigning the flow, because that was what
the brief said. The tickets were sitting there the whole time. Now I read the
support queue before I open Figma, on every project — it is the cheapest
research available and almost nobody does it.

I'd also push harder on the date. "5–7 working days" is a hedge, and two of five
participants read it as "seven." A real date, even a conservative one, would
have been better, and I don't think I made that case as well as I could have.
