## The problem

The product was a supply-chain analytics tool, and its main screen was a table:
up to forty columns, a few thousand rows, read for six hours a day by planners
who knew their data better than we ever would. The existing table had been built
column by column over three years. Nine type sizes, four shades of grey doing
three different jobs, and row heights that changed depending on whether a cell
happened to contain a badge.

Users didn't complain about how it looked. They complained about *missing
things* — a planner would scan a column of numbers and not notice the one that
had gone negative.

## Constraints

The table had to stay dense. Every attempt to give it more breathing room had
been rejected by users, loudly: fewer rows on screen meant more scrolling, and
scrolling is where you lose your place. No virtualization rewrite either — the
existing grid worked and was not on the roadmap. I could change type, colour,
spacing, and borders. That was the whole surface.

## What I did

I printed the table. Actual paper, actual planners, three of them, with a
highlighter each: mark every cell you'd look at first, and every cell you had to
hunt for.

The hunted-for cells had a pattern. They were almost always numbers that needed
comparing down a column, and the thing stopping the comparison was that the
digits didn't line up. The table used the UI font, which had proportional
figures, so `1,113` was narrower than `8,008` and the columns wobbled.

## Decisions, and why

**Tabular figures everywhere a number appears.** One CSS declaration —
`font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums` — and every numeric column became a clean
vertical edge you could run your eye down. This is the single highest-value
change I have ever made to a screen and it took four minutes.

**Right-align numbers, left-align text, no exceptions.** The old table aligned
by whatever looked balanced per column. Consistency mattered more than balance:
once the rule was absolute, planners stopped re-reading headers to work out what
kind of column they were in.

**One row height. One.** Badges got smaller and lost their padding rather than
pushing a row taller. A uniform 32px row means your eye tracks horizontally
without correction, and it makes the whole grid feel machined instead of grown.

**Three greys, each with a job.** Ink for values, muted for labels and units,
line for borders. The other greys were doing emphasis work, which is what the
one accent colour is for. Every negative number now carries the accent, and
because nothing else on the screen does, the thing users kept missing is the
only thing that moves.

**Borders only where the eye needs a rail.** Full gridlines make a table look
like a spreadsheet and add forty vertical lines of visual noise. I kept
horizontal rules at a very low contrast and dropped verticals entirely; the
tabular figures were already doing that job.

## Outcome

Time-to-find on the standard task — locate the three worst-performing SKUs in a
region — dropped from a median of 41 seconds to 12 in a nine-person test. The
density stayed identical: same rows on screen, to the pixel.

The table shipped as a component, and the rules became the four paragraphs of
our design system that people actually quote back at me.

## What I'd change

I did the type and colour work first and the alignment rules last, which is
backwards. Alignment fixed more than colour ever did, and if I'd started there I
would have needed fewer colour changes at the end.

I also never solved horizontal scrolling. At forty columns you are always
somewhere in the middle with no idea where, and a sticky first column is a
plaster, not a fix. That's the next project, and I don't yet know the answer.
